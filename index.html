A · Executive summary

The order Zomato loses is the one the user wanted most

Product

Zomato food delivery (Eternal Ltd.)

Industry

Food delivery, consumer internet, India

Problem

Occasional Explorers can't judge individual dishes at restaurants they haven't tried, so they abandon discovery and fall back to familiar orders or leave.

Target users

Occasional Explorers: 1–2 orders a week, open to new restaurants, rarely act on it

Business opportunity

Every first order at a new restaurant is a chance to raise order frequency, the metric Zomato names as its primary growth driver

Key insight

Users trust "500 people ordered this" more than a 4.2-star rating. All five interviewees raised this unprompted; 21 of 24 survey respondents agreed. Research

Proposed solution

A dish-trust system: a one-tap "Would you reorder?" prompt after delivery, bestseller tags with order counts on restaurant cards, and a Dish Signal Layer (photo, order count, reorder %) on every menu item.

Expected impact

Higher conversion when users view a new restaurant, leading to more orders per user per month. Target to be set after a baseline read. Hypothesis

My product thesis

  1. Zomato's next growth comes from frequency, not just new users. With over 58% share of food delivery order value, the bigger lever is getting existing users to order more often. Fact
  2. The highest-intent moment is the most fragile. A user browsing a new restaurant is ready to spend, and also one bad signal away from leaving.
  3. Trust is decided at the dish, not the restaurant. A restaurant rating can't tell you whether its biryani is good. Users want dish-level proof. Research
  4. The best signal is one users create by ordering. Order counts and "would reorder" votes are harder to fake than stars, and Zomato already has the data.
  5. Fix the product problem before the operations one. Fast delivery scored highest, but its root cause sits in logistics. Dish trust is fully within product's control.

05 · Root cause analysis

The problem isn't missing photos. It's missing proof.

5 Whys

  1. 1
    Why do Explorers abandon new restaurants? They can't tell whether a dish will be good.
  2. 2
    Why can't they tell? The only signal is a restaurant-level star rating, which says nothing about one dish.
  3. 3
    Why isn't the rating enough? Users see stars as gameable ("stars can be bought"), and a good restaurant can have a bad dish.
  4. 4
    Why is there no dish-level signal? Feedback is collected at restaurant level, and order data is never shown to users.
  5. 5
    Root cause: Zomato doesn't turn its own dish-level order data and post-order feedback into proof users can see at the moment of choice.

Contributing causes

Effect: users can't judge a dish at an unfamiliar restaurant

Data shown

Feedback loop

  • Rating prompt appears while users unpack, and most dismiss it
Trust environment
  • Sponsored results at the top erode trust

Why I selected this problem

On scores alone, fast delivery wins by one point. I didn't pick it, for three reasons.

  1. Ownership. Fast delivery depends on kitchen prep times, rider density and dark-store-style logistics. A product team can't fix it alone. Dish trust is a product problem end to end.
  2. Leverage at peak intent. Dish trust fails at the Decision stage, when the user has already chosen a restaurant and is ready to pay. Fixing the last step of an intended order converts the most valuable sessions.
  3. Compounding value. Every delivered order adds data to the signal. Speed has to be paid for on every order; trust gets better on its own.

Fast delivery stays on the roadmap as an operations-led workstream. Mood-based entry and dish-level price filters are adjacent problems, tracked separately so the P0 stays focused.

09 · Product solution

A dish-trust system that gets smarter with every order

Research → Insight (order counts beat stars) → Problem (no dish proof) → Opportunity (show existing data) → Prioritization (P0) → Solution

Solution overview

1 · Data engine

"Would you reorder?"

One yes/no tap after delivery. The answers become a reorder % on each dish.

2 · Before the menu

Bestseller tags on cards

The top 2 dishes, with order counts, shown on the restaurant card in search and listings.

3 · At the decision

Dish Signal Layer

Photo, order count, reorder % and a "Most ordered" tag on every menu item.

Why this solution

It uses data Zomato already owns, matches what users said they trust, and needs no new content from restaurants to start.

How it solves the root cause

It turns hidden order data and post-order feedback into visible, dish-level proof at the moment of choice.

User benefit

Pick a dish at a new restaurant in seconds, without Googling it or falling back on the usual.

Business benefit

More conversions on new-restaurant views, more variety, more orders per user, and a trust asset rivals can't copy quickly.

Risks, trade-offs and constraints

  • Risk: popular isn't always good. Tags need a minimum volume (50+ orders in 30 days) and are removed if dissatisfaction on a tagged dish passes 15%.
  • Risk: restaurants faking orders. Flag clusters of orders from the same device or IP; freeze the tag pending review.
  • Trade-off: new restaurants and dishes start with no signals. Accepted for MVP; a "New on Zomato" label avoids penalizing them.
  • Trade-off: no composite "Dish Score". Users asked for raw numbers they can interpret, not another rating to trust.
  • Constraint: menu card space. Signals must fit without pushing prices or add buttons off-screen.
10 · Product requirements

Lightweight PRD: Dish Signal Layer v1

ObjectiveIncrease conversion when Occasional Explorers view a restaurant they haven't ordered from
User storyAs an Occasional Explorer, I want to see how many people ordered a dish and would order it again, so I can pick a new dish with confidence

Functional requirements

  1. After delivery, show one prompt per order: "Would you order [dish] again?" Yes / No. Dismissible; shown at most once per order.
  2. Compute per dish: orders in the last 30 days and reorder % (yes ÷ all responses).
  3. Show order count on a menu item only when it has 50+ orders in 30 days; show reorder % only with 20+ responses.
  4. Tag the top dish per restaurant (by 30-day orders, above threshold) as "Most ordered".
  5. Show the top 2 dishes with order counts on restaurant cards in listings and search.
  6. Label dishes and restaurants below the thresholds "New on Zomato" rather than showing zero.

Non-functional requirements

  • Signals refresh at least daily; menu load time increases by no more than 100 ms.
  • Counts are rounded ("500+ orders") to protect restaurant data and avoid false precision.
  • Works in all supported app languages and on low-end Android devices.

Acceptance criteria

  • Given a dish with 50+ orders in 30 days, when the user opens the menu, then the order count shows under the dish name.
  • Given a dish below threshold, then no count shows and no "0 orders" text appears.
  • Given a user answers the reorder prompt, then the response counts within 24 hours and the prompt does not appear again for that order.

Edge cases

  • Dish renamed or repriced: keep history if the item ID is unchanged; reset if it's a new item.
  • Restaurant with very few orders: show no signals, only "New on Zomato".
  • Cancelled or refunded orders: excluded from counts and from the prompt.
  • Group orders with many dishes: ask about at most 2 dishes, chosen by price.
Dependencies
  • Order data pipeline at dish level
  • Notifications team (prompt)
  • Trust & Safety (fraud rules)
  • Restaurant partner comms
Constraints
  • Restaurant agreements on data shown
  • Menu UI space
  • Cold start for new dishes
Success criteria
  • Statistically significant lift in new-restaurant conversion vs control
  • No rise in post-order dissatisfaction

12 · MVP definition

What ships first, and what waits

In the MVP

  • "Would you reorder?" prompt
  • Order counts on menu items (above threshold)
  • "Most ordered" tag
  • Top 2 dishes on restaurant cards
  • "New on Zomato" label
  • Basic fraud rules (device/IP clusters)

Not in the MVP

  • Verified buyer photos
  • Trending near you feed
  • AI review summaries
  • Composite dish score
  • Mood-based homepage
  • Fast-delivery tier

Why: the MVP uses only data Zomato already has, plus one tap from users. It tests the core bet, that dish proof lifts conversion, at the lowest cost. Photos and AI summaries add value but need new content pipelines and moderation; they come after the bet is proven.

What this project taught me

  • Research should be allowed to change your mind. Order counts weren't in my Phase 1 list. Users brought it up on their own, and it became the core of the solution.
  • Workarounds are the strongest evidence. When users Google a dish before ordering, the product has already failed them.
  • The highest score isn't always the right P0. Owning the root cause matters as much as the size of the pain.
  • Problems stack. Meera's session didn't fail at one point. Three small frictions added up to "just order the usual."

A · Executive summary

Rapido's biggest risk isn't price or supply. It's broken bookings.

Product

Rapido: bike taxis, autos, cabs and delivery

Industry

Ride-hailing, two-sided mobility marketplace, India

Problem

After a captain accepts, the ride often doesn't happen as booked: the captain calls to ask the destination and cancels, demands extra cash, or holds out for a better fare. Research

Target users

Daily commuters, the riders who ride most often and have the least slack in their schedule

Business opportunity

Rivals copied the zero-commission model in 2025. Reliability is now the open way to win the rider's first tap. Fact

Key insight

The captain is behaving rationally. On a flat daily fee, every ride is a business decision, and the product makes backing out after accepting free. Hypothesis

Proposed solution

One-tap rider report with instant priority rebooking, full trip details before a captain accepts, and reliability-based dispatch that gives better rides to captains who keep their word.

Expected impact

A higher share of booked rides completed at the app fare, more weekly rides from commuters, and steadier captain renewals. Hypothesis

My product thesis

  1. Rapido's model is both its advantage and its weak spot. Captains keep the whole fare, which drew supply, and also gives them full control over which rides actually happen.
  2. Price parity changes the game. When every app charges captains a flat fee, low fares stop being a reason to choose Rapido. Certainty becomes the reason.
  3. Rapido's real customer is the captain, but the flywheel starts with riders. Revenue comes from captain subscriptions, and captains subscribe only if riders open Rapido first.
  4. Fix the incentive, not the person. Fines would push captains to rival apps, one tap away. Rewards for reliability keep the model intact.
  5. The moment that matters is right after "accept." That's when the app currently has no lever, and where a small rule change has the most effect.

05 · Root cause analysis

The captain isn't the problem. The rules at the moment of accept are.

5 Whys

  1. 1
    Why do booked rides fail? Captains cancel or renegotiate after accepting.
  2. 2
    Why do they cancel after accepting? They learn the trip isn't worth their time or fuel, often only after calling the rider.
  3. 3
    Why does the trip's value matter so much? On a flat daily fee, the captain keeps the whole fare, so every ride is a profit decision.
  4. 4
    Why is backing out after accepting easy? The system treats a post-accept cancellation much like any other outcome; there is no lasting cost or reward.
  5. 5
    Root cause: Accepting carries no commitment. The product neither gives captains full information before they accept nor rewards them for keeping bookings.

Why I selected this problem

  1. It hits the most valuable rider at the worst moment. Commuters ride most often, and a break after "accepted" is when they've stopped looking elsewhere.
  2. It's a product problem, not a pricing one. Peak-hour matching needs changes to fares and supply. Reliability after accept needs only better rules and information at one step.

09 · Product solution

Make keeping a booking the most profitable choice

Research → Insight (backing out is free) → Problem (accept ≠ commit) → Opportunity (reward kept bookings) → Prioritization → Solution

One-tap report + instant rebook

"Captain asked me to cancel" or "asked for extra" logs it against the captain, not the rider, and rebooks the rider with priority.

Full trip on the request card

Drop point, distance and exact earnings shown before accept, so there's no reason to call and ask.

Reliability-based dispatch

Captains who keep bookings get first pick of high-value and peak requests.

How it solves the root cause

It adds commitment to "accept": captains decide with full information, and keeping the booking earns them better rides.

User benefit

Accepted rides happen; when they don't, a new ride arrives fast without arguing.

Business benefit

More completed rides, more commuters opening Rapido first, and more value for captains' daily fee.

Captain benefit

Reliable captains earn more per hour from better-paying rides.

Risks, trade-offs and constraints

  • Risk: false reports. A report counts only when it matches a cancellation or a call before pickup; review happens only after repeated reports from different riders.
  • Risk: captains decline more upfront. Fuller trip details may lead to more declines on short trips. Track decline rate and short-trip fulfilment; if they fall, test a short-trip boost.
  • Trade-off: rewards over penalties. Slower to change behaviour than fines, but it keeps supply on the platform.
  • Trade-off: peak boost waits. Shipping it first could teach captains to hold out for boosts.
  • Constraint: regulation. Fare rules differ by state, and bike taxis face policy risk; the solution must work for autos and cabs too.
10 · Product requirements

Lightweight PRD: Kept Booking v1

ObjectiveIncrease the share of accepted rides completed at the app fare, for commuters in launch cities
User storyAs a daily commuter, I want a captain who accepts my ride to actually take me at the fare shown, so I can plan my morning around it

Functional requirements

  1. After a captain accepts, the rider sees a "Problem with this ride?" option with: "Captain asked me to cancel", "Captain asked for extra money", "Captain isn't moving".
  2. Choosing an option cancels the ride without penalty to the rider, logs it against the captain, and starts a priority rebook within 5 seconds.
  3. The captain's request card shows drop area, distance, estimated time and exact earnings before accept.
  4. Each captain gets a rolling 14-day reliability score: completed ÷ accepted, adjusted for verified reports.
  5. Phase 2: dispatch offers high-value and peak requests first to captains above a reliability threshold.
  6. Captains see their reliability score and how to improve it.

Non-functional requirements

  • Rebooking adds no more than 5 seconds before matching starts; reports work offline and sync later.
  • Reliability scores update at least hourly; scoring rules are explainable to captains in one screen.
  • Works on low-end Android phones and in all supported languages.

Acceptance criteria

  • Given a rider reports "asked me to cancel", then no cancellation fee applies to the rider and a new search starts immediately.
  • Given a captain's ride is reported, then it's counted against reliability only if it matches a cancellation or a pre-pickup call.
  • Given a captain opens a request, then drop area and earnings are visible without scrolling.

Edge cases

  • Genuine captain emergencies (breakdown, accident): a captain-side "unable to continue" reason, reviewed separately and not scored.
  • Rider changes destination after pickup: fare updates in-app; not a reportable "extra money" case.
  • New captains with few rides: neutral score until 20 accepted rides.
  • Repeated reports from one rider against many captains: rider flagged for review.

Constraints
  • State fare regulations
  • Captain supply at peak
  • Must not add fines
Success criteria
  • Higher fulfilment rate vs control cities

What this project taught me

  • A business model creates its own problems. The zero-commission model that built Rapido is the same thing that makes bookings fragile.

  • Separate what you know from what you think. Without interviews, the honest output is a sharp hypothesis and a plan to test it.
  • Experiments in marketplaces need different designs. A simple user A/B test would give misleading results here.

Why an SQL database

Purchases are naturally relational: a payment belongs to an invoice, which belongs to a PO, which belongs to a vendor. A relational database enforces those links; a spreadsheet can't.

User benefit

Answers about orders, bills and dues in seconds, without asking anyone.

Business benefit

Fewer payment errors, a clear view of spend by vendor, and data to negotiate with.

  • Constraint: data entry discipline. The system is only as good as what people enter, so every screen was kept short.
  • Risk: falling back to spreadsheets. If entering a PO is slower than writing it down, people stop. Speed of entry was a design priority.

Summary

Two workflows replace the most repetitive part of a sales day

Problem

Every lead needed a researched opening line, a sent email, up to two follow-ups on the right days, and a check for replies. Done by hand, follow-ups slipped and replies sat unnoticed.

Workflow 1

Every weekday at 10:00: pick up to 40 new leads, write a personal first line with AI, send, and log it.

Workflow 2

Every weekday at 11:00: check each thread for replies, alert the team, or send the next follow-up in the same thread.

Outcome

Outreach and follow-ups run on schedule without manual sends, and no reply goes unnoticed.

01 · The problem

The cost of manual outreach isn't sending. It's remembering.

02 · Design decisions

Choices that made it reliable, not just automatic

Google Sheet as the source of truth

The team already lived in sheets. Keeping leads there meant no new tool to learn, and anyone could see a lead's status.

Two workflows, not one

Sending and following up run on different clocks. Splitting them keeps each simple and lets one fail without stopping the other.

AI writes one line, not the email

The AI writes only the opening line, from facts in the sheet. The rest is a fixed template, so the message stays accurate.

Follow-ups in the same thread

Replying in-thread gives the prospect context and looks like a person following up, not a new campaign.

Stop the moment someone replies

A reply ends the sequence and alerts a human. Automation handles the chase, people handle the conversation.

Slow and capped on purpose

40 emails a day and a 90-second gap between sends protect the sender's reputation and keep emails out of spam.

06 · Guardrails

What keeps the automation safe and respectful

  • Easy opt-out: every first email says to reply "no" to stop; the team marks Do Not Contact, and the filter excludes it forever.
  • Daily cap and pacing: 40 emails a day, 90 seconds apart, to avoid spam filters and protect the sending domain.
  • No invented facts: the AI is told to use only the sheet's inputs and write one line; the template carries the claims.
  • Fallback copy: if the AI call fails, a human-written opener is used instead of an empty line.
  • Replies go to people: automation never answers a reply; it hands over.
  • No secrets in the files: Gmail, Sheets and OpenAI credentials live in n8n's credential store, not in the workflow code.

Build email (Code node, workflow 1)

// Combine the lead's details with the AI-written opening line into a ready-to-send email.
const lead = $('One lead at a time').item.json;
const aiLine = ($json.choices?.[0]?.message?.content || '').trim().replace(/^"|"$/g, '');

// If the AI call returns nothing, fall back to a safe opener written by a human.

Decide next step (Code node, workflow 2)

Detects replies and applies the follow-up timing rules.

// For each contacted lead: did they reply? Is a follow-up due? Is the sequence finished?
const lead = $('One lead at a time').item.json;
const messages = $json.messages || [];
const OUR_ADDRESS = 'YOUR_SENDING_EMAIL'; // the Gmail address the outreach is sent from

// A reply is any message in the thread that we didn't send.
const replied = messages.some((m) => {
  const from = (m.payload?.headers || []).find((h) => h.name === 'From')?.value || '';
  return !from.toLowerCase().includes(OUR_ADDRESS.toLowerCase());
});

const step = Number(lead.Step || 1);
const lastTouch = new Date(lead['Last Touch']);
const daysSince = Math.floor((Date.now() - lastTouch.getTime()) / 86400000);

// Sequence rules: follow-up 1 after 3 days, follow-up 2 after 4 more, close after 5 more.
const WAIT_DAYS = { 1: 3, 2: 4 };
const MAX_STEP = 3;
const CLOSE_AFTER_DAYS = 5;

const followUps = {
  2: `Hi ${lead['First Name']}, following up on my note below. ` +
     'Happy to share how a similar brand set this up, if that would help.',
  3: `Hi ${lead['First Name']}, I'll close the loop here. If after-sales support ` +
     'becomes a priority later, just reply and I\'ll pick it up.',
};

const nextStep = step + 1;
const sendFollowUp = !replied && step < MAX_STEP && daysSince >= WAIT_DAYS[step];
const finished = !replied && step >= MAX_STEP && daysSince >= CLOSE_AFTER_DAYS;

return {
  json: {
    email: lead.Email,
    firstName: lead['First Name'],
    company: lead.Company,
    threadId: lead['Thread ID'],
    lastMessageId: messages.at(-1)?.id,
    replied,
    sendFollowUp,
    followUpBody: followUps[nextStep] || '',
    nextStep,
    nextStatus: finished ? 'No response' : 'Contacted',
  },
};

What this project taught me

  • Automate the remembering, not the relationship. The value was never in sending faster; it was in never missing a follow-up or a reply.
  • Use AI where it's safe. One researched line is high value and low risk; a fully AI-written pitch is the opposite.
  • Build on the tools people already use. Keeping the Google Sheet meant the team adopted it on day one.
  • Design for failure. Fallback copy, caps and pauses mattered as much as the happy path.
